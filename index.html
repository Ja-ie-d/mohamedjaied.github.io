      Capstone project · Sep – Dec 2025
      
PQC vs QKD in Practice

      
Quantum computers will one day break the cryptography that protects today's internet. There are two leading answers to that threat. I built both, joined them into one hybrid key, and measured how well they hold up.

      

        
Timeline4 months

        
Keys labeled844

        
Keys validated400

        
StackPython · Qiskit

      

    


    

      
Chapter 1
The threat

      

        
Most secure connections today agree on a key using RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography. A large enough quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve the math behind both. Data recorded now could be decrypted later, so the switch to quantum-safe methods has to start before such machines exist.

        
Two families of solutions compete for that job. My question was simple: which one works in practice?

        

          

            Answer A · Physics
            
Quantum Key Distribution

            
Send the key as photons using the BB84 protocol. Measuring a quantum state disturbs it, so an eavesdropper leaves fingerprints in the error rate.

          

          
vs

          

            Answer B · Mathematics
            
Post-Quantum Cryptography

            
Replace RSA with math that quantum computers can't shortcut. Kyber512 uses lattice problems and runs on ordinary hardware.

          

        

      

    


    

      
Chapter 2
Catching a spy

      

        
The core of the QKD side is a Qiskit simulation of BB84 with basis sifting and Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER) modeling. Alice sends qubits in random bases, Bob measures in random bases, and they keep only the positions where their bases matched.

        
If someone intercepts and resends the qubits, they guess the wrong basis half the time, which corrupts about a quarter of the sifted bits. Try it yourself:

        

          

            
            
          

          

            
Sifted bits251

            
Errors0

            
QBER0.0%

          

          
11% abort threshold

          
Error rate is low. Alice and Bob keep the key.

        

        
In my simulation, every key came from the Qiskit circuit below. Across all 844 keys the average QBER was 4.09%, ranging from 0.36% to 15.64%, and sifting kept 50.0% of bits, exactly what BB84 predicts.

        
        
      

    


    

      
Chapter 3
One hybrid key

      

        
Instead of betting on one answer, the platform combines both. The sifted BB84 key and the Kyber512 shared secret are fed together into SHAKE-256, an extendable-output hash function, to derive a single hybrid key.

        

          

            
QKDBB84 sifted key

            
PQCKyber512 shared secret

          

          

          
DeriveSHAKE-256

          

          
ResultHybrid key

        

        
An attacker now has to break both inputs. If one method fails in the future, the key still rests on the other.

        
Every hybrid key then went through the platform's quality checks: Shannon entropy, a chi-square randomness test and the frequency (monobit) test, combined into one quality score.

        

          
          
        

      

    


    

      
Chapter 4
Quantum speedup

      

        
The platform uses Grover's quantum search algorithm to speed up key validation. Grover finds a marked item among N possibilities in about √N steps, a quadratic speedup over checking them one by one. I used it to search for key-quality patterns across 400 keys.

        
        
The same speedup is why Grover matters for security. A quantum attacker could search 2n keys in about 2n/2 steps. Drag the slider to see what that does to key strength.

Chapter 5
Teaching a model to judge keys

      

        
A key is only as good as its randomness. The standard check is the NIST SP 800-22 statistical test suite, which is slow to run on every key. So I trained a model to predict the result.

    2. 
          
  2. Test each one with NIST SP 800-22 and record whether it passes.
    3. 
          
  3. Train a calibrated Random Forest on 844 labeled keys.
    4. 
          
  4. Validate on 400 new keys against real NIST results.

Chapter 6
Head to head

      

        
The study compared the two approaches on security, scalability and deployment cost.

        

          

            
PQC · Kyber512QKD · BB84

            
              
Security rests onMath problems believed hard for quantum computersLaws of physics; eavesdropping shows up as errors

              
ScalabilityWorks over existing networks at internet scaleNeeds a quantum channel; distance is limited

              
Deployment costA software upgrade on current hardwareDedicated photon sources, detectors and links

            
          

        

        
The hybrid design takes the practical reach of PQC and adds the physical guarantees of QKD where a quantum link exists.

Keep it on campus
